Are Teenage Girls Treated as Public Property?

An examination of how digital panopticons, informal policing, and institutional indifference have normalized the public ownership of young women’s private lives.

By Shaunav Kaushik

There is a question that is difficult to shake after watching how quickly strangers, neighbours and self-appointed moral guardians can turn a young woman’s ordinary life into a matter for public inspection.

When a girl walks down a road, sits in a park, speaks to a boy, dresses in a particular way or appears in a photograph, the reaction around her can sometimes suggest that her existence comes with a public terms-and-conditions page. Other people feel entitled to inspect it, interpret it and, in the most disturbing cases, police it.

The problem is larger than individual incidents

The legal questions raised by such incidents matter. So do the social questions. Who is allowed to intervene in another person’s private life? What gives an individual the authority to act as an informal police officer? And why can the language of morality become a shield for conduct that would otherwise be recognized as plainly abusive?

These questions become especially serious when the person being watched, confronted or humiliated is a minor. A teenager's age does not make her public property. Her privacy, dignity and bodily autonomy remain matters that institutions are expected to take seriously.

Law and the psychology of informal policing

Law is often discussed as if the only relevant actors are courts, police officers and legislatures. Everyday power is more complicated. Social norms can create their own systems of surveillance. A group can decide that it has the right to question someone. A stranger can decide that he is entitled to demand an explanation. A bystander can decide that intervention is justified because something "looks wrong."

Understanding the psychology of this informal policing matters because it helps explain why certain behaviours can become normalized long before an institution formally intervenes.

What should change?

The answer begins with a basic distinction: concern for another person does not create ownership over that person. Institutions should respond to concrete wrongdoing through lawful processes, while individuals should be cautious about turning personal assumptions into authority.

For girls and young women, that distinction is not abstract. It determines whether an ordinary moment remains ordinary or becomes an invitation for strangers to take control of it.

Privacy is not a reward for behaving correctly. It is part of being treated as a person.

Legal Codes will return to the legal, psychological and social structures that make these ordinary violations possible — and to the question of what meaningful accountability should look like.
